Abstract

The exponential expansion of contemporary academic literature imposes an intense cognitive burden on researchers, necessitating automated summarization tools that distill dense paper abstracts into salient, bite-sized scientific highlights. Unlike standard narrative summaries, research highlights require high information density, strict factual fidelity, and structural conciseness. In this investigation, we conduct an extensive empirical benchmark on the FIRE MixSub-SciHigh dataset—encompassing 13,825 scientific publications across multi-disciplinary domains—to evaluate four distinct architectural paradigms: (1) Google Pegasus-PubMed (specialized Seq2Seq encoder-decoder), (2) BioMistral-7B (domain-adapted biomedical causal LLM), (3) Qwen-2.5-Instruct (high-efficiency instruction-tuned causal LLM), and (4) Meta Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (frontier general-purpose LLM). To move beyond one-dimensional surface-overlap evaluations, we formulate a holistic Three-Pillar Evaluation Framework spanning: (i) Reference Quality and LCS alignment (ROUGE-L F1, Target Lexical F1), (ii) Source Lexical Grounding and Extrinsic Hallucination Risk Proxy ($P_{\text{source}}$, Ungrounded-Token Risk %), and (iii) Inference Efficiency (Wall-Clock Latency per sample, Throughput). Experimental findings reveal distinct performance trade-offs: BioMistral-7B establishes decisive factual superiority with 84.44% vocabulary grounding and a minimal 15.56% hallucination risk proxy (reducing novel extrinsic vocabulary by over 60% relative to general LLMs). Concurrently, Qwen-2.5-Instruct achieves the highest reference quality (19.87% ROUGE-L F1, 28.27% Target F1) while delivering unmatched inference speed (3.31 s/sample; 28.19 tokens/sec), operating 2.4× faster than BioMistral and Llama 3.1, and 3.2× faster than Pegasus. Finally, we translate these findings into an open-source, interactive dual-engine dashboard (Streamlit and Gradio) equipped with real-time visual token-grounding verification to assist digital libraries and peer-review systems.

Keywords: Scientific Highlight Generation, Natural Language Processing, Large Language Models, FIRE MixSub-SciHigh, Factual Grounding, Hallucination Mitigation, BioMistral, Qwen-2.5.

1. Introduction

In contemporary science and engineering, the volume of scholarly literature published each year exceeds human reading capacity. Digital indexing databases index hundreds of thousands of new articles monthly. While research abstracts have long served as primary syntheses, they frequently contain background context, broad problem motivation, and introductory framing that obscures the underlying experimental breakthrough. To facilitate rapid knowledge discovery, prominent scientific publishers (including Elsevier, Springer Nature, and IEEE) now mandate Research Highlights: an author-curated sequence of three to five core, high-impact bullet statements summarizing novel discoveries, empirical findings, and methodological innovations.

Automating scientific highlight generation presents distinct challenges compared to standard news or narrative text summarization:

  1. High Information Density & Technical Jargon: Abstracts encompass domain-specific biochemical nomenclatures, mathematical variables, and complex pharmacological relationships.
  2. Severe Penalty for Factual Hallucinations: In life sciences and clinical medicine, generating an ungrounded or contradictory claim (e.g., claiming a drug reduced mortality when the abstract noted it increased toxicity) can have dangerous real-world consequences.
  3. Structural Granularity: Highlights require discrete bullet points that synthesize disparate quantitative outcomes rather than rephrasing the abstract into a shorter continuous paragraph.

Historically, scientific summarization relied upon supervised Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) encoder-decoder transformers such as Pegasus [1] pre-trained with Gap Sentences Generation (GSG) objectives. Recently, the NLP landscape has transitioned to decoder-only Autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs) possessing billions of parameters [2, 3, 4]. However, whether general-purpose frontier LLMs, domain-specialized foundation models, or lightweight instruction-tuned architectures offer the optimal balance of quality, factual fidelity, and computational runtime remains an open empirical question.

Core Research Contributions:

  • A Standardized Multi-Model Benchmark: We systematically evaluate four distinct architectural families—Pegasus-PubMed, BioMistral-7B, Qwen-2.5-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct—under strict uniform constraints on the FIRE MixSub-SciHigh corpus.
  • The Three-Pillar Evaluation Framework: We formulate an evaluation protocol measuring: (1) Reference Quality, (2) Source Lexical Grounding and Extrinsic Risk Proxy, and (3) Inference Latency and Throughput.
  • Identification of Pareto-Optimal Paradigms: We uncover empirical trade-offs, demonstrating that BioMistral-7B provides peerless factual safety, whereas Qwen-2.5 provides superior generation speed and journal-ready bullet formatting.
  • Interactive Deployment System: We engineer a complete dual-engine web application (Streamlit and Gradio) featuring live token-level visual hallucination audits for real-time peer review.

2. Related Work

Scientific Document Summarization: Early scientific text summarization focused on extractive methods utilizing graph-based ranking algorithms (e.g., TextRank, LexRank). With the advent of deep contextual embeddings, abstractive summarization gained prominence. Specialized transformer architectures, notably Pegasus-PubMed [1] and SciBERT, were pre-trained on large academic corpora (arXiv and PubMed) utilizing gap-sentence mask filling to mimic human summarization behavior. While effective on continuous text, these models struggle with structured, bullet-delimited highlight syntax.

Domain-Adapted Foundation Models: Adapting foundation models to specialized verticals has proven critical for technical disciplines. BioMistral [2] demonstrated that initializing from Mistral-7B and continuing self-supervised pre-training on PubMed Central open-access papers significantly improved biomedical reasoning and medical licensing question benchmarks. Concurrently, instruction-tuned architectures such as Qwen-2.5 [3] and Meta Llama 3.1 [4] have demonstrated superior zero-shot prompt following, enabling models to generate structurally constrained outputs without parameter fine-tuning.

Hallucination Detection in Generative NLP: Generative hallucinations represent a central bottleneck in deploying LLMs. Extrinsic hallucinations—where the model introduces information neither supported nor contradicted by the source—are pervasive in abstractive systems. Prior literature has evaluated factual consistency through entity matching, dependency parsing, and Natural Language Inference (NLI) entailment models. In this work, we deploy normalized set-theoretic lexical precision as an automated proxy to measure the degree of vocabulary overlap directly against the source abstract.

4. The Three-Pillar Evaluation Framework

Conventional summarization research frequently focuses exclusively on ROUGE overlap. However, in scientific production environments, a model scoring high lexical overlap might introduce fabricated medical claims or exhibit impractically high generation latency. To address this, we establish a comprehensive Three-Pillar Evaluation Framework:

4.1 Pillar 1: Reference Quality (Human Highlight Alignment)

Pillar 1 measures structural and semantic similarity between the model's generated text ($G$) and the author-written gold reference highlight ($R$).

ROUGE-L (Longest Common Subsequence): Evaluates sentence-level structure by identifying the longest common subsequence of tokens between generated and reference strings, preserving relative token order:

$$\text{Precision}_{LCS} = \frac{LCS(G, R)}{|G|}, \qquad \text{Recall}_{LCS} = \frac{LCS(G, R)}{|R|}$$ $$\text{ROUGE-L}_{F1} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision}_{LCS} \cdot \text{Recall}_{LCS}}{\text{Precision}_{LCS} + \text{Recall}_{LCS}}$$

Target Lexical F1: To supplement n-gram sequencing, we compute normalized whitespace-separated bag-of-words set overlap after case-folding and punctuation stripping:

$$P_{\text{target}} = \frac{|G_{\text{tokens}} \cap R_{\text{tokens}}|}{|G_{\text{tokens}}|}, \qquad R_{\text{target}} = \frac{|G_{\text{tokens}} \cap R_{\text{tokens}}|}{|R_{\text{tokens}}|}$$ $$F1_{\text{target}} = \frac{2 \cdot P_{\text{target}} \cdot R_{\text{target}}}{P_{\text{target}} + R_{\text{target}}}$$

4.2 Pillar 2: Source Lexical Grounding & Extrinsic Hallucination Risk Proxy

Pillar 2 audits whether the generated text remains anchored in the source abstract ($S$) or invents unverified concepts:

Source Lexical Precision ($P_{\text{source}}$): The fraction of generated unique vocabulary tokens that directly appear in the source abstract:

$$P_{\text{source}} = \frac{|G_{\text{tokens}} \cap S_{\text{tokens}}|}{|G_{\text{tokens}}|}$$

Ungrounded-Token Risk Proxy (%): The complement of source precision, quantifying the proportion of novel, non-abstract vocabulary introduced by the model:

$$\text{Risk}_{\text{proxy}} (\%) = (1.0 - P_{\text{source}}) \times 100$$

Interpretation: While not an absolute semantic fact-checker, a lower ungrounded proxy indicates that the model strictly preserves the terminology of the abstract rather than extrapolating speculative vocabulary.

4.3 Pillar 3: Inference Speed & Deployment Feasibility

Pillar 3 measures wall-clock runtime efficiency under identical hardware conditions:

Mean Generation Latency ($\overline{T}$): Average wall-clock seconds consumed per document generation pass:

$$\overline{T} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \quad (\text{seconds per sample})$$

Sample and Token Throughput:

$$\text{Throughput}_{\text{sample}} = \frac{1}{\overline{T}} \quad (\text{samples/sec}), \qquad \text{Throughput}_{\text{token}} = \frac{\sum \text{Tokens Generated}}{\sum T_i} \quad (\text{tokens/sec})$$

5. Experimental Setup & Benchmarked Models

5.1 Model Architectures Under Study

  1. Google Pegasus-PubMed (google/pegasus-pubmed): 568M parameter encoder-decoder transformer pre-trained on scientific biomedical articles using GSG objectives.
  2. BioMistral-7B (BioMistral/BioMistral-7B): 7B parameter causal LLM adapted from Mistral-7B through continuous pre-training on PubMed Central open access corpora.
  3. Qwen-2.5-Instruct (Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct / Qwen2.5-7B): Advanced instruction-tuned causal LLM developed by Alibaba Cloud, optimized for structured formatting and high-throughput reasoning.
  4. Meta Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct): 8B parameter flagship open-weights foundation model trained on over 15 trillion tokens with extensive alignment.

5.2 Uniform Experimental Controls

To ensure scientific fairness, identical tokenization ceilings and hardware constraints were enforced across all evaluation runs:

  • Input Token Ceiling (MAX_SOURCE_LENGTH): Fixed at 512 tokens to avoid truncation asymmetry.
  • Output Budget (MAX_TARGET_LENGTH): Fixed at 128 tokens, enforcing compact highlight length.
  • Hardware Environment: NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (15.84 GB VRAM) hosted on standard Google Colab environments.
  • Quantization Protocol: 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) quantization via bitsandbytes with double quantization, reducing causal LLM memory footprints from ~16 GB to under 5.5 GB VRAM without catastrophic performance degradation.
  • Prompt Formatting: Causal models were guided with a standardized prompt: "You are an expert scientific editor. Generate concise, accurate scientific highlights summarizing the core findings of the provided abstract."

6.1 Detailed Pillar-by-Pillar Analysis

Pillar 1 (Reference Quality): Autoregressive decoder models systematically outperformed the classical Seq2Seq model (Pegasus-PubMed: 16.90% ROUGE-L) by approximately 3 percentage points. Qwen-2.5-Instruct achieved the highest ROUGE-L F1 (19.87%) and Target F1 (28.27%), closely shadowed by BioMistral-7B (19.80% ROUGE-L F1, 27.37% Target F1). The difference between Qwen and BioMistral is marginal (Δ = 0.07%), demonstrating that both models capture the core semantic substance of author highlights with equal proficiency.

Pillar 2 (Factual Grounding & Hallucination Mitigation): BioMistral-7B demonstrated unprecedented factual grounding. An overwhelming 84.44% of its generated tokens existed verbatim within the source abstract, yielding an ungrounded risk proxy of only 15.56%. In stark comparison, general foundation models (Llama 3.1: 46.60% risk; Qwen 2.5: 40.51% risk) exhibited ungrounded rates nearly three times higher. This constitutes empirical validation that continued pre-training on specialized biomedical corpora establishes strict boundaries on vocabulary hallucination.

Pillar 3 (Inference Efficiency): Qwen-2.5-Instruct established complete dominance in computational throughput, generating highlights in 3.31 seconds per sample at 28.19 tokens/sec. This is 2.4× faster than BioMistral (8.04 s) and Llama 3.1 (7.90 s), and 3.2× faster than Pegasus (10.56 s). For digital library indexing pipelines processing thousands of papers, Qwen delivers substantial economic and operational advantages.

11. Limitations & Threats to Validity

  1. Lexical Proxy vs. Semantic Entailment: Set-based lexical precision evaluates token overlap rather than deep semantic entailment. A clinically valid synonym (e.g., substituting "neoplasm" for "tumor") is penalized as ungrounded, whereas a negated claim using identical tokens receives high overlap. Future work should integrate claim-level Natural Language Inference (NLI).
  2. Hardware Bounds & Quantization: Models were quantized to 4-bit NormalFloat4 (NF4) to accommodate standard 16 GB VRAM GPUs. While NF4 preserves foundational performance, slight perplexity degradation relative to full 16-bit precision is expected.
  3. Zero-Shot Evaluation: Benchmark passes evaluated zero-shot instruction capabilities. Supervised parameter-efficient fine-tuning (QLoRA) on the full 10,000 training set is expected to further enhance ROUGE-L scores.

12. Conclusion & Future Directions

In this research, we formulated and executed a rigorous Three-Pillar Comparative Benchmark on the FIRE MixSub-SciHigh dataset, comparing classical Seq2Seq and modern causal LLMs across Quality, Factual Grounding, and Inference Speed. Our findings demonstrate that BioMistral-7B provides unparalleled factual safety (84.44% grounding, 15.56% risk proxy), making it the premier choice for clinical and biomedical document synthesis. Simultaneously, Qwen-2.5-Instruct proves optimal for large-scale production, delivering top ROUGE-L quality (19.87%), native bullet syntax, and 2.4× faster execution (3.31 s/sample).

Future directions include conducting full QLoRA fine-tuning across the 10,000 training pairs, submitting blind predictions on the 1,840 masked test set, and deploying automated claim-level entailment verification via LLM-as-a-judge frameworks.
